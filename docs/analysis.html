How many Democrats back Medicare for All?

Every Democratic candidate on the November ballot, and one marked exception, measured three ways — what they publish, what they have signed, and what has been reported — and the large gap between the first two.

First, what the number is out of

Any “share who support X” figure is only as good as the share of candidates you actually managed to read. So that comes first.

Reading campaign websites alone left 153 candidates unaccounted for — more than a third of the field, and not a random third, since the sites that defeat a crawler belong disproportionately to well-funded incumbents. Three further sources closed almost all of it.

Three kinds of evidence, not one

89 candidates cosponsor H.R.3069, the Medicare for All Act — a recorded legislative act. 252 stated a position on their own campaign site. 63 more were resolved from news coverage, and 24 by hand where the site serves a crawler almost nothing. Where they conflict, the stronger evidence wins, and every row in the tracker records which source decided it.

164 candidates support Medicare for All or single-payer

37.6% of all 436 on the ballot support Medicare for All or single-payer, counting the bill’s cosponsors alongside those who say so publicly.
17.4% counting campaign websites only 75 candidates. The measure of what they choose to tell voters, and under half the real figure.

The distance between those two numbers is the most interesting thing in this dataset, and it is almost entirely incumbents: 64 of the 89 cosponsors of the Medicare for All Act never mention it on their own campaign site.

Signing the bill and campaigning on it are close to disjoint activities. A reader who judged the House Democratic caucus by its campaign websites would conclude that support is under half what the congressional record shows.

33 candidates have no coverage position we could establish anywhere. That is not silence on health — many write at length about cancer research, mental health, reproductive care or veterans’ services. They simply take no position on the insurance question.

Almost no candidate argues against Medicare for All in so many words. The 239 in the middle are not campaigning against it — they are campaigning on something else, usually protecting the ACA, cutting drug costs or defending Medicaid.

The distinction the classifier exists to catch

“I support Medicare for All, which would remove the profit motive from health insurance and allow us all to access quality care, for a lower price.”
William Lawrence · MI-07 Supports Medicare for All
“Allowing Iowans the option to buy into Medicare at any age — which will increase competition, expand choice, and bring down costs — is the best way to do it.”
Christina Bohannan · IA-01 Does not support Medicare for All

Both sentences contain the word “Medicare.” Only the first is Medicare for All. Keyword counting collapses them — and collapses a great deal else besides, all of it in the same direction, toward overstating support.

So every match is read in the context of its sentence before it counts. A statement that is negated (“I don’t support Medicare for All”), attributed to someone else (“my opponent supports…”), or held at arm’s length (“while I admire the goal…, we should start with a public option”) does not register as support. Every classification carries the verbatim quote and the rule that fired, so any row can be checked by hand in the tracker.

What this cannot tell you

  • Twenty seats are not in the field yet. Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Delaware had not voted when this was collected. Louisiana holds no nominating primary at all in 2026 — its all-party ballot is the November election, after House races were postponed following Louisiana v. Callais.
  • “Does not support” means a stated position that is not Medicare for All — a public option, ACA measures, or explicit opposition. It is never inferred from silence: a candidate with nothing stated is counted under No position found.
  • The last bucket merges two different facts. “Read it, states no coverage position” and “could not read it” are not the same thing. The finer seven-tier classification is kept per candidate in the tracker and the CSV.
  • A campaign website is not a voting record, and a voting record is not a campaign. The two measures answer different questions, which is why both are reported rather than blended.
  • A sitting member has to have signed the bill. Cosponsoring H.R.3069 costs a member nothing but says everything, so for incumbents the Medicare for All tiers require it. Where a campaign line or a press profile describes a member as a supporter and their name is not on the bill, the claim is dropped rather than replaced: whatever else their own material says stands, and if it says nothing they read as no position rather than as opposition. Challengers are not held to this — they have no bill to sign. One member was affected.
  • Two rows are marked exceptions. Robert White is running for the District of Columbia’s non-voting delegate seat, which is not one of the 435 voting districts; he is the Democratic nominee to succeed the retiring Eleanor Holmes Norton. And Alaska’s at-large seat has no Democrat contesting it in earnest: the party’s only name on the November ballot is a perennial filer serving a federal sentence, who reached the general with 3.8% of the primary vote after the actual Democratic candidate withdrew and endorsed the independent, Bill Hill. Hill is counted here in his place, and recorded with his real party in the data. Both rows carry an asterisk wherever they appear.
  • Louisiana comes from the state, not from Wikipedia. It holds no nominating primary in 2026 — the all-party ballot is the November election — so the roster there is the Secretary of State’s certified list of who qualified, and nothing else. That replaced two sources wrong in opposite directions: Wikipedia’s “declared” lists, which over-count and mislabel party, and a campaign-finance fallback, which caught only candidates who raised enough to appear in a finance table.
  • News-sourced positions are the weakest evidence here. They carry a confidence score, and thin or contradictory cases are labelled as such rather than smoothed over.
  • The roster is audited against federal filings. Every name is checked against the FEC’s candidate master file for its seat, and every seat with active Democratic filers is checked back against the roster. 427 of 435 match; the eight that do not are flagged in the data rather than quietly dropped. A missing filing is not always an error: a candidate only registers with the FEC once they raise or spend $5,000, so several late Louisiana entrants have none.
