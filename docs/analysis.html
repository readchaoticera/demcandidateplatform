How many Democrats back Medicare for All?

Every Democratic candidate on the November ballot, measured three ways — what they publish, what they have signed, and what has been reported — and the large gap between the first two.

First, the denominator

What the number is out of

Any “share who support X” figure is only as good as the share of candidates you actually managed to read. So that comes first.

Reading campaign websites alone left 92 candidates unaccounted for — more than a fifth of the field, and not a random quarter, since the sites that defeat a scraper belong disproportionately to well-funded incumbents. Two further sources closed almost all of it.

Three kinds of evidence, not one

89 candidates cosponsor the bill — a recorded legislative act. 257 stated a position on their own campaign site. 70 more were resolved from news coverage, and 13 by hand where the site serves a crawler almost nothing. Where they conflict, the stronger evidence wins, and every row in the Explorer records which source decided it.

The answer

162 candidates support Medicare for All or single-payer

37.5% of all 432 on the ballot back Medicare for All or single-payer, counting the bill’s cosponsors alongside those who say so publicly.
17.4% counting campaign websites only 75 candidates. The measure of what they choose to tell voters, and under half the real figure.

The distance between those two numbers is the most interesting thing in this dataset, and it is almost entirely incumbents: 64 of the 89 cosponsors of the Medicare for All Act never mention it on their own campaign site.

Signing the bill and campaigning on it are close to disjoint activities. A reader who judged the House Democratic caucus by its campaign websites would conclude that support is less than half what the congressional record shows.

Even after adding every source, 48 candidates have no coverage position we could establish. That is not silence on health — many write at length about cancer research, mental health, reproductive care or veterans’ services. They simply take no position on the insurance question.

Almost no candidate argues against Medicare for All in so many words. The 222 in the middle bucket are not campaigning against it — they are campaigning on something else, usually protecting the ACA, cutting drug costs or defending Medicaid.

How a candidate is scored

The distinction the tiers exist to catch

“I support Medicare for All, which would remove the profit motive from health insurance and allow us all to access quality care, for a lower price.”
William Lawrence · MI-07 Medicare for All
“Allowing Iowans the option to buy into Medicare at any age — which will increase competition, expand choice, and bring down costs — is the best way to do it.”
Christina Bohannan · IA-01 Public option

Both sentences contain the word “Medicare.” Only the first is Medicare for All. Keyword counting collapses them — and collapses a great deal else besides, all of it in the same direction, toward overstating support.

So every match is read in the context of its sentence before it counts. A statement that is negated (“I don’t support Medicare for All”), attributed to someone else (“my opponent supports…”), or held at arm’s length (“while I admire the goal…, we should start with a public option”) does not register as support. Every classification above the bottom tier carries the verbatim quote and the rule that fired, so any row can be checked by hand in the Explorer.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

  • Twenty seats are not in the field yet. Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Delaware had not voted when this was collected. Louisiana holds no nominating primary at all in 2026 — its all-party ballot is the November election, after House races were postponed following Louisiana v. Callais.
  • A campaign website is not a voting record, and a voting record is not a campaign. The two measures answer different questions, which is why both are reported rather than blended.
  • “Does not support” means a stated position that is not Medicare for All — a public option, ACA measures, or explicit opposition. It is never inferred from silence: a candidate with no stated position is counted in the third bucket instead.
  • The last bucket merges two different facts. “Read it, states no coverage position” and “could not read it” are not the same thing. The finer seven-tier classification is kept per candidate in the Explorer and the CSV.
  • Some sites serve a crawler almost nothing. Where a campaign runs a Javascript-rendered site, the served HTML can carry no policy text at all. Those rows are corrected by hand, recorded in config/overrides.yaml with a source and a named reviewer, and marked “human review” in the Explorer.
  • News-sourced positions are the weakest evidence here. They carry a confidence score, and thin or contradictory cases are labelled as such in the Explorer rather than smoothed over.
  • No incumbency breakdown. That field comes from the FEC, which was skipped for want of an API key, so incumbents and challengers cannot be compared directly — though cosponsorship is a close proxy.
