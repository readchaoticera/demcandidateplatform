First, the denominator

What the number is out of

Any “share who support X” figure is only as good as the share of candidates you actually managed to read. So that comes first.

Reading campaign websites alone left 105 candidates unaccounted for — a quarter of the field, and not a random quarter. Two further sources closed almost all of it.

Three kinds of evidence, not one. 89 candidates cosponsor the bill — a recorded legislative act. 263 stated a position on their own site. 77 more were resolved from news coverage. Where they conflict, the stronger evidence wins, and every row records which source decided it.

The answer

153 candidates support Medicare for All

35.7% of the 429 with a known position support Medicare for All, counting the bill's cosponsors alongside those who say so publicly.
18.9% counting campaign websites only 62 candidates. The measure of what they choose to tell voters, and less than half the real figure.

The distance between those two numbers is the most interesting thing in this dataset. It is almost entirely incumbents: 69 of the 89 cosponsors of the Medicare for All Act never mention it on their own campaign site.

Signing the bill and campaigning on it are close to disjoint activities. A reader who judged the House Democratic caucus by its campaign websites would conclude that support for Medicare for All is less than half what the congressional record shows.

Even after adding every source, 113 candidates have no position on how coverage should work. That is not silence on health — many write at length about cancer research, mental health, reproductive care or veterans’ services. They simply take no position on the insurance question.

Not one candidate, in any source, argued against Medicare for All. Among Democrats the disagreement isn’t for or against — it’s between naming it and not raising the subject.

How a candidate is scored

The distinction the tiers exist to catch

“I support Medicare for All, which would remove the profit motive from health insurance and allow us all to access quality care, for a lower price.”
William Lawrence · MI-07 Explicit M4A
“Allowing Iowans the option to buy into Medicare at any age — which will increase competition, expand choice, and bring down costs — is the best way to do it.”
Christina Bohannan · IA-01 Public option

Both sentences contain the word “Medicare.” Only the first is Medicare for All. Keyword counting collapses them — and collapses a great deal else besides, all of it in the same direction, toward overstating support.

So every match is read in the context of its sentence before it counts. A statement that is negated (“I don’t support Medicare for All”), attributed to someone else (“my opponent supports…”), or held at arm’s length (“while I admire the goal…, we should start with a public option”) does not register as support. Every classification above the bottom tier carries the verbatim quote and the rule that fired, so any row can be checked by hand.

California fields 60 Democrats across 52 districts — more candidates than seats, because its top-two primary can send two Democrats to the same general election. Eight of its districts do. It also has by far the most Medicare for All support in absolute terms: 34 candidates, more than twice any other state.

Limits

What this cannot tell you

  • Twenty seats are not in the field yet. Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Rhode Island and Delaware had not voted when this was collected. Louisiana holds no nominating primary at all in 2026 — its all-party ballot is the November election, after House races were postponed following Louisiana v. Callais.
  • The 105 unreadable candidates are the largest source of error, and they are not random: sites built on modern page builders render only in a browser, so a whole class of newer, better-funded campaigns is underrepresented.
  • A campaign website is not a voting record. This measures what candidates choose to publish. A sitting member may co-sponsor the Medicare for All Act and never mention it on their homepage, and would be scored here on the homepage.
  • No incumbency breakdown. That field comes from the FEC, which was skipped for want of an API key, so incumbents and challengers cannot be compared.
  • 49 classified rows are flagged for review — cases the rules could not settle confidently. They are listed separately rather than folded silently into the totals.
